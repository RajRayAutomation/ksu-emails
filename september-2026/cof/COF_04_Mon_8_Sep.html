Good morning. One of the most significant shifts in neuroscience over the last decade has not been about the brain at all. It has been about the gut.

A researcher called Dr John Cryan at University College Cork has been mapping the communication pathways between the gut microbiome and the brain for fifteen years. What he has found has changed the way we think about mood, cognition, anxiety and even personality.

The gut-brain axis is a bidirectional communication highway. The gut sends signals to the brain via the vagus nerve, through hormones, through immune signalling and through the neurotransmitters produced by gut bacteria. Approximately 90% of the body's serotonin is produced in the gut. Your mood is, in part, a reflection of your gut microbiome health.

The science

The vagus nerve and the gut-brain communication system

The vagus nerve is the tenth cranial nerve and the primary communication channel between the gut and the brain. It carries signals in both directions, but the majority of traffic — approximately 80% — travels from gut to brain rather than brain to gut. Gut bacteria influence this signalling through the production of short-chain fatty acids, neurotransmitter precursors and direct activation of enteroendocrine cells that communicate with vagal afferents. Dysbiosis produces altered vagal signalling that manifests as mood changes, anxiety, cognitive fog and altered stress responses.
