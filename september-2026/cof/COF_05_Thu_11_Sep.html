Mycotoxins. Mould-derived toxic compounds that form on coffee beans during growing, harvesting, processing or storage when conditions are insufficiently controlled. The two most relevant to coffee are ochratoxin A and aflatoxin B1.

Less than 1% of commercially available coffee undergoes third-party testing for mycotoxin contamination. The regulatory limits were set based on what was technologically achievable, not on what is biologically optimal. There is a difference.

The science

What mycotoxins do and why low-level chronic exposure matters

Ochratoxin A is a nephrotoxin and immunosuppressant. At the chronic low doses found in commercial coffee, the research suggests associations with systemic inflammation, immune dysregulation, gut permeability changes and in some populations, neurological effects. Aflatoxin B1 is a potent hepatotoxin and classified carcinogen. Regulatory limits allow its presence in food at levels considered acceptable for population exposure. Whether daily consumption of coffee containing sub-limit mycotoxin levels constitutes meaningful biological burden is genuinely contested in the research literature. What is not contested is that testing for it and removing it is better than not.

Purity Coffee tests every batch for mycotoxins, pesticide residues, heavy metals and acrylamide. Not as a marketing claim. As the baseline standard of what clean coffee actually means. The testing results are available. The sourcing is traceable.

Fun fact: The specific growing conditions that minimise mycotoxin formation include high altitude — because lower temperatures reduce mould growth rates — wet processing — because it removes the cherry pulp that harbours mould — and rapid, controlled drying. Aponte Inga meets all three criteria. Most commercial coffee meets none of them.
