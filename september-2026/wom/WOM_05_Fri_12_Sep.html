Progesterone has a direct sedative effect mediated through GABA — the brain's primary inhibitory neurotransmitter. As progesterone declines in perimenopause, GABAergic activity decreases. The result is easy sleep onset but early-morning waking and inability to return to deep sleep. It is not insomnia. It is hormonal.

The science

The GABA-progesterone connection and why it matters for sleep

Progesterone is converted in the brain to allopregnanolone, a potent positive modulator of GABA-A receptors — the same receptors targeted by benzodiazepines. When progesterone is adequate, this natural sedative effect supports sustained deep sleep. When it falls, the GABAergic sedation diminishes and the brain becomes more susceptible to arousal during the lighter sleep phases that naturally occur in the early morning hours. Cortisol, which should be at its nadir between midnight and 3am, is often elevated in women with HPA axis dysregulation — which itself is worsened by declining progesterone.
