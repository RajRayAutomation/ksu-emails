Picture a woman we will call Clare. Fifty one, busy, capable. Every November she feels her mood dip as soon as the evenings go dark.

She used to think it was just her.

Seasonal changes in mood are common, and they are more noticeable when other things are changing too.

The science

Light, mood and hormones

Less daylight changes how much serotonin and melatonin your body makes, which can affect mood, energy and sleep. During perimenopause and menopause, fluctuating hormones can add to the picture, so the winter dip may feel stronger than it used to. None of this is weakness. It is physiology, and it responds to simple things: daylight, movement, company and good sleep.

Small, steady actions help far more than dramatic ones. Get outside in the morning. Move your body in a way you enjoy, even if it is only a walk. Eat regular meals with plenty of protein and plants. See friends, even when you would rather stay in. And be honest with your GP if low mood lingers, because there is a lot of support available and you do not have to wait until it is unbearable.

Perimenopause and menopause can make the winter dip feel like a double blow, but understanding why helps. It is not you failing. It is a body under more load than usual, in a season that asks for more. A kind, practical plan is the best response.
