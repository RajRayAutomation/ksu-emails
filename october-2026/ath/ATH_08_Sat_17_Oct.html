October is the month when training injuries cluster. Not randomly. Predictably. The same injuries. The same athletes. The same week of the month.

A runner called Sam. 43 years old. Every autumn he developed Achilles tendinopathy around week three of October. His training load had not spiked dramatically. The temperature had dropped. That was enough.

Cold tendons are less elastic than warm ones. The Achilles, already the most loaded tendon in distance runners, absorbs significantly more impact force in cold conditions where the plantar fascia stiffens and transfers load upward. Sam was not training harder. His tissue was less forgiving.

The science

Why October injures tendons

Tendon tissue responds to temperature in ways muscle tissue does not. Collagen fibres, which give tendons their tensile strength, become less extensible below 15 degrees. The critical issue is not that cold tendons tear, they rarely do under normal load, but that they transmit force differently, absorbing less and transferring more to insertion points and neighbouring structures. The IT band, Achilles and hamstring attachments are the three sites where this shows up most reliably in October. Adding 10 minutes of warm-up before any October session, not a light jog but deliberate dynamic movement to raise tissue temperature, reduces the injury incidence significantly.

Fun fact: Research from sports medicine clinics shows that tendon-related injuries in distance runners peak in October and April, the two transition months when training load is rising and tissue temperature is changing rapidly. Both are predictable and largely preventable with appropriate warm-up and load management.
