Cold air is denser than warm air. Denser air contains more oxygen molecules per litre. Your lungs, processing the same volume of air, extract more oxygen in October than they do in July. The result is a measurable improvement in aerobic capacity that arrives every autumn and is almost entirely squandered by athletes who treat October as a holding pattern rather than a building opportunity.

A runner called Priya. 38 years old. Her personal bests were almost exclusively set in October and November. She had assumed this was psychological. It was physiological. Cold air was giving her lungs a seasonal advantage that she had never been taught to deliberately exploit.

The science

The oxygen density advantage and how to exploit it

At 10 degrees Celsius, air density is approximately 3% higher than at 25 degrees. For an endurance athlete running at threshold pace, that 3% represents a meaningful increase in oxygen delivery to working muscles per breath. Combined with the reduced risk of overheating that makes October sessions more comfortable at higher intensities, the result is a genuine aerobic development window that opens every autumn and closes in January when the benefits of adaptation plateau. The athletes who push into October rather than back off it emerge in spring with cardiovascular ceilings they could not have built in summer heat.

The role of ketones in this window is to ensure your cellular machinery is ready to use the additional oxygen being delivered. Mitochondrial efficiency determines how much of the oxygen arriving at the cell membrane actually becomes ATP. Fun fact: The world records for marathon running are set almost exclusively in October and November at events in Berlin, Chicago, New York and Valencia. The temperature range of 8 to 12 degrees is not a coincidence. It is the aerobic sweet spot.
