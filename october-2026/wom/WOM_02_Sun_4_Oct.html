Why October impairs thyroid function in perimenopausal women

Three factors converge in October that collectively suppress thyroid efficiency. First, cortisol elevation from the seasonal HPA axis upregulation directly inhibits the conversion of T4 to the active T3 form. Second, falling temperatures slow metabolic rate, which alters the demand signal the body sends to the thyroid. Third, reduced daylight suppresses TSH production through a mechanism involving the suprachiasmatic nucleus and the hypothalamic-pituitary-thyroid axis. Any one of these factors would produce a modest effect. Together in October they produce the clinical picture Judith presented with every year.

Fun fact: TSH, the pituitary hormone that drives thyroid activity, shows measurable seasonal variation in population studies, with the lowest values in summer and highest in winter. The autumn transition period shows the most rapid rate of change. Women with borderline thyroid function are most vulnerable during this shift.
