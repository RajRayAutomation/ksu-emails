The vagus nerve, the primary communication channel between gut and brain, is profoundly sensitive to cortisol. When cortisol is elevated, vagal tone decreases. The two-way communication between gut and brain becomes less efficient. The gut becomes more reactive. The brain receives more stress signals from the gut, which generates more cortisol, which further reduces vagal tone.

October cortisol elevation creates this cycle reliably in a significant proportion of people. They notice it as digestive changes, bloating that was not there in August, a heaviness after meals, lower energy after eating. The gut-brain axis is running on a stressed signal.

The science

How elevated cortisol affects the vagus nerve and gut-brain communication

Cortisol reduces heart rate variability, the measure most associated with vagal tone, through direct action on the autonomic nervous system. Lower vagal tone means the parasympathetic nervous system, responsible for rest, digest and recovery, is less dominant relative to the sympathetic system. In the gut, reduced parasympathetic tone slows gastric motility, impairs digestive enzyme secretion and increases gut permeability by reducing mucosal barrier function. The practical result is that digestion is measurably worse in high-cortisol states, and the gut sends more inflammatory signals upward through the vagus nerve to the brain.

Fun fact: Vagal tone, measured as heart rate variability, is 12 to 18% lower in winter than summer in population studies, with the steepest decline occurring in October. Higher vagal tone is consistently associated with better gut function, lower inflammatory markers and better cognitive performance. October is the month to actively support it.
